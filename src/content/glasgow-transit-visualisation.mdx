I've lived in Glasgow for almost seven years. I still find myself surprised, on a fairly regular basis, by how difficult it is to get somewhere by public transit. Not because the services aren't there, but because the experience of using them feels like it was never really designed to be an experience at all. You check one app for the train, another for the bus, discover the tickets don't transfer between them, and end up walking a stretch you shouldn't have to walk. It's death by a thousand small inconveniences.

Coming from Hamburg, where a single ticket covers the U-Bahn, S-Bahn, buses, and ferries as one integrated network, this took some getting used to. Hamburg isn't a special case either. Integrated ticketing is pretty standard across most of continental Europe. But standing on a Glasgow platform, looking at a map that only shows the little circle of the Subway and nothing else, it's hard not to feel like someone just decided the city wasn't worth the effort.

![Hamburg's U and S-Bahn network](/hamburg-public-transit.png)

![Glasgow Subway](/glasgow-subway.png)

The thing is, Glasgow has a lot more to work with than the Subway map suggests. Outside of London, it has the largest suburban rail network in the UK. Lines threading out across the city and into the surrounding region, the Argyle and North Clyde lines running right through the city centre, an extensive bus network on top of all that. The raw infrastructure is not the problem. The problem is that none of it presents itself as a coherent whole. There's no map that shows all of it. No ticket that covers all of it. No moment where you can look at Glasgow and understand it as a city with a transit system, rather than a city with several transit-adjacent services that happen to overlap.

I'm not the first person to notice this. Campaigns like [Get Glasgow Moving](https://www.getglasgowmoving.org/) have been making the political case for years. But I wanted to do something simpler and more concrete: just make it visible.

## What I built

The starting point was departures, which felt like the most tangible unit of a transit network. Not routes in the abstract, but actual trains leaving actual stations across the course of a day. I took Glasgow Central and Glasgow Queen Street, the two main rail hubs, and visualised their departures over time: which lines, how often, where the gaps are, how the rhythm of the network changes from morning rush to late evening.

Data comes from [Transitland](https://www.transit.land/), an open platform that aggregates GTFS feeds from transit agencies worldwide. GTFS is the standard format agencies use to publish their schedules, and Transitland's API lets you query departures for a given stop across a time window, and separately pull the geographic coordinates of each route. That means the lines on the map follow the actual geometry of the track rather than a straight-line guess between stops.

![Departures at Glasgow Central and Queen Street](/overview-departures-top-level.png)

The same approach works for the Subway. Its departures tell a different story, a tighter and more predictable loop, and sitting alongside the suburban rail view the contrast starts to show you something about how Glasgow's network is actually structured, and where it isn't.

![Departures on Glasgow Subway](/glasgow-subway-departures.png)

The goal was never to produce a report or a set of recommendations. It was just to make something you can look at and feel the shape of the network: where it's busy, where it goes quiet, how these services that don't officially belong to the same system are in fact all serving the same city. Glasgow's transit problem is partly political and partly infrastructural, but it's also a legibility problem. The network is hard to understand as a whole because no one has ever really had to present it as one. This is a small attempt at that.

## Where can you actually get to?

Departures show you when things leave. They don't tell you where those things can take you, which turns out to be a harder and more interesting question.

I found myself thinking about this in terms of a specific scenario. You're somewhere in Glasgow, at a particular station, right now. You have thirty minutes. Where can you actually go? Not in theory, not if everything runs on time and you plan in advance, but given the services that exist and are running, where does the network actually reach from where you're standing?

The answer, I suspected, was more than most people realise — and less connected than it should be.

The reachability view lets you pick any Glasgow rail or subway stop and see every destination reachable within thirty minutes, based on real-time departure data. The dots on the map are coloured by how long it takes to get there: green for under ten minutes, amber for twenty, orange approaching the half-hour limit. The route lines show you the actual paths — not as the crow flies, but the geometry of the track.

![Reachability from Partick with route maps](/partick-example.png)

What surprised me when I started using this wasn't the destinations themselves but the shape of how they cluster. From most of the southern suburban stations, the thirty-minute radius extends surprisingly far east and north through the city centre, while whole neighbourhoods a short distance away remain completely out of reach. The network has a grain to it, and the reachability view makes that grain visible in a way that a static route map doesn't.

The more revealing discovery came from allowing transfers. A single transfer — changing from one service to another with at least five minutes to make the connection — opens up significantly more of the city. The suburban rail and the Subway operate as separate systems, with separate tickets and no formal integration, but they share physical space at a handful of stations. Partick is the most obvious: the rail station and the Subway entrance are a short walk apart, and a transfer there connects two networks that otherwise have no relationship to each other.

![Transfer-inclusive results from Charing Cross, with route and leg detail](/including_transfers.png)

When you see those transfer paths listed out — board here at 22:03, arrive there at 22:15, change platforms, continue on a different service — it becomes clear that the network is more navigable than it presents itself as being. The information exists. The connections are physically possible. What's missing is any official acknowledgement that they exist, any integrated ticketing that makes using them feel like a single journey rather than two separate ones you happened to stitch together.

That gap between what the network can do and what it tells you it can do feels like the most honest summary of Glasgow transit I've found. The infrastructure is there. The legibility isn't.
