Deprivation in Scotland isn't invisible. You can feel it in the difference between a high street that has a Greggs and a Wetherspoons and nothing else, and one that has a farmers' market on Saturday morning. You can see it in the gap between the school that has a running track and the one that's been sharing a PE kit for a decade. But feeling it and measuring it are different things. The Scottish Index of Multiple Deprivation is an attempt to close that gap — to make something that everyone already knows exists into something you can actually look at.

SIMD 2020 covers all of Scotland divided into 6,976 data zones, each containing roughly 500 to 1,000 people. For each zone the Scottish Government has calculated scores across seven domains: income, employment, health, education, access to services, crime, and housing. The overall index is a weighted combination of all seven, but the interesting thing is not the overall rank. The interesting thing is what happens when you pull those domains apart.

## What I built

The application is a choropleth map of Scotland coloured by any of 37 indicators drawn from the SIMD indicators file. The default is income deprivation rate — the percentage of people in each zone receiving income-related benefits — but you can switch to any indicator from the dropdown in the header, and the map re-colours instantly.

![Scotland coloured by income deprivation rate](/deprivation-overview.png)

A key decision was how to handle the colour scale. The naive approach is to map the data's minimum and maximum values to the two ends of a green-to-red gradient. In practice this produces a map that is almost entirely one colour, because most indicators in a deprivation dataset are heavily right-skewed. Drug-related hospital stays are a good example: the median data zone has a standardised ratio of around 50, well below the Scottish average of 100, but the top end reaches 1,800 — eighteen times the national average. A linear scale built on that range would paint 90% of Scotland in shades of green and say almost nothing useful.

The fix is quantile-based colouring. Instead of asking where a value falls on the number line between the minimum and maximum, you ask where it falls in the ranked distribution of all values. The zone at the 50th percentile always gets yellow, the top 20% always get red. The full spectrum is used regardless of how skewed the data is.

Data comes from two sources: the SIMD 2020v2 indicators file published by the Scottish Government, and the Data Zones 2011 boundary shapefile. The shapefile is in British National Grid projection and covers Scotland at a resolution that produces a file size of around 140 MB before simplification. The backend processes it once — reprojecting to WGS84, simplifying the geometry to roughly a tenth of its original size — and caches the result. Everything is served from a FastAPI backend; the frontend is React with Leaflet.

Clicking any data zone opens a panel showing all 37 indicator values for that zone, with the currently selected indicator highlighted at the top. The contrast between neighbouring zones is often where the data becomes most legible — two zones a street apart can tell very different stories.

![Glasgow zoomed in, with Woodlands selected](/woodlands-overview.png)

## What the data shows

The access domain is the one that surprises me most. Rural deprivation and urban deprivation look completely different when you start separating the indicators. A data zone on the outskirts of Glasgow might score well on access — GP surgery three minutes away, a retail centre five — but badly on income, health, and crime. A zone in the Highlands or on an island might have almost no income deprivation at all but a drive time of forty-five minutes to a GP and no public transport option worth the name.

The drive and public transport times for the same destination often tell very different stories. For a post office, the median drive time across Scotland is around three minutes. The median public transport time is closer to thirty. That gap is the structural assumption that Scotland's services are built around car ownership, made visible as a number.

The health indicators carry the data that's hardest to look at. Scotland has one of the highest rates of drug-related deaths in Europe, and the DRUG indicator — hospital stays related to drug use, expressed as a standardised ratio where 100 is the Scottish average — shows the geography of that crisis clearly. The zones above 500 are not scattered randomly. They cluster, mostly in the same post-industrial and peripheral urban areas that also score highly on unemployment, poor health, and low attainment.

Deprivation compounds. Paisley Ferguslie — one data zone in Renfrewshire — has an income deprivation rate of 50%, meaning half of its 480 residents receive income-related benefits. Its comparative illness factor is 360, more than three and a half times the Scottish average. Drug-related hospital stays are at 477. The crime rate is 799 per 10,000 people. School leaver attainment is 4.7, against a national median closer to 6. University entry for 17 to 21 year olds is 1.6%. These numbers don't exist in isolation. They describe the same community from seven different angles, and they all point in the same direction.

![Paisley Ferguslie — all indicators](/paisley-overview.png)

That's what the index is designed to show, and it does. What you can do with this map is compare. Pick income deprivation and look at the shape of Scotland. Then switch to school attendance and see whether it follows the same pattern, or diverges. Some of the access indicators — particularly public transport times — show a completely different geography from the deprivation indicators, spreading the worst values out into areas that in every other dimension look relatively comfortable. That friction between maps is where most of the interesting questions live.
